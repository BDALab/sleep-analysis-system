\abstract{%
\textbf{Background / introduction:} Wrist actigraphy records movement during
everyday life and may help characterise sleep and activity changes in mild
cognitive impairment with Lewy bodies (MCI-LB). We examined associations with
clinical outcomes in healthy controls (HC) and MCI-LB and evaluated whether
these measurements could distinguish the groups.

\textbf{Methods:} A classifier for sleep and wake was developed from two
external wearable datasets using polysomnography as the reference. We then analysed 132 visits from
118 people in two closed Czech clinical collections, each with five to seven
valid nights. We calculated 260 sleep and diary features and an extended set of
690 features including activity measures. Spearman correlations were screened
with control of the false discovery rate, and similar features were grouped
into families. The primary clinical association analysis used generalised estimating equations (GEE) adjusted
for diagnosis, sex, and education and clustered visits by person. Secondary
classification compared XGBoost with elastic net logistic regression using
nested validation with five folds and participants kept separate between folds.
We also evaluated performance within each data source and compared it with a
model using recruitment group alone.

\textbf{Results:} Greater activity variability was associated with higher motor
scores (adjusted beta 1.322 points per feature standard deviation, 95\% CI
0.807--1.837) and lower attention scores (beta $-0.198$, 95\% CI
$-0.311$ to $-0.084$). More variable long awakenings were associated with
lower executive scores (beta $-0.155$, 95\% CI $-0.254$ to $-0.056$).
Wake bout frequency, sleep efficiency, and wake after sleep onset were
associated with REM sleep behaviour symptom scores specifically in MCI-LB. The broad
XGBoost classifier achieved ROC AUC 0.746 (95\% CI 0.652--0.830).

\textbf{Conclusions:} Activity variability and sleep continuity measured in everyday life
show interpretable associations in HC and MCI-LB and merit independent
validation.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) is commonly preceded by a heterogeneous
predementia phase in which cognitive, motor, sleep, autonomic, and psychiatric
features can emerge before dementia. Research criteria describe mild cognitive
impairment with Lewy bodies, as well as presentations beginning with delirium
or psychiatric symptoms, while emphasising the need for longitudinal validation of early
markers \cite{mckeith2020prodromal}. Idiopathic rapid eye movement (REM) sleep
behaviour disorder is an important prodromal synucleinopathy phenotype
\cite{postuma2019risk}. Definitive diagnosis requires polysomnography in a
sleep laboratory, whose resource and access demands constrain widespread
use \cite{zhou2025multicenter}.

Wrist actigraphy is inexpensive and well suited to repeated
measurement. Previous studies have shown that actigraphy can contribute to
screening for idiopathic REM sleep behaviour disorder \cite{zhou2025multicenter, stefani2018screening},
that measures of rest and activity over 24 hours differ in this population
\cite{winer2023restactivity}, and that sleep, physical activity, and circadian
features derived from accelerometry relate to symptoms beyond motor impairment
\cite{ophey2025accelerometry}. Recent multicentre work also demonstrates both
the promise and the difficulty of generalising actigraphy models across devices
and populations \cite{zhou2025multicenter}. Two issues therefore need to be
addressed together: whether engineered wearable features have clinically
interpretable associations, and whether diagnostic performance remains credible
when validation keeps participants separate and accounts for data sources.

The present exploratory study focused on healthy control (HC) and mild
cognitive impairment with Lewy bodies (MCI-LB) visits assembled from multiple
Czech research data sources. We first screened a broad set of sleep, diary, and activity features
against motor, sleep behaviour, fluctuation, visuospatial, attention, and
executive outcomes. To reduce the risk of interpreting many correlated
variants as independent discoveries, we mapped features to fixed conceptual
families, grouped highly similar variants, and examined representative
features using regression that accounted for repeated visits from the same person.
We then asked whether the same
feature families supported HC versus MCI-LB classification using nested
validation that kept participants separate, with further evaluation within each data source.

These analyses answer distinct questions. The primary analysis using
generalised estimating equations (GEE) asks whether
wearable features are associated with clinical outcomes after accounting for
repeated visits and measured covariates. The secondary classification analysis
asks whether HC and MCI-LB can be separated when no participant appears in both
training and validation data. Analyses within each source ask whether the
classification results hold across recruitment settings. The elastic net
comparison asks whether a simpler linear model captures the signal as well as
XGBoost, which can represent nonlinear relationships.

The computational contribution is a framework for phenotyping and validation
that explicitly accounts for data sources. It combines:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item grouping hundreds of correlated features from several nights into interpretable families,
  \item estimating clinical associations while accounting for repeated visits from the same person,
  \item comparing XGBoost and elastic net using nested validation, with checks using first visits, separate data sources, and recruitment group alone.
\end{enumerate}
This design evaluates how recruitment affects model performance and distinguishes
exploratory clinical associations from evidence for diagnostic prediction.

\subsection{External datasets for sleep/wake model development}\label{subsec:sleep-model-data}

The initial development source was the Newcastle PSG and accelerometer dataset
(Zenodo record 1160410) \cite{vanhees2018newcastle}. It contains a single night
of PSG from 28 patients attending a sleep clinic, PSG sleep scores every 30 seconds,
and 55 GENEActiv files recorded from both wrists at 85.7~Hz (one file from the right wrist
was unavailable) \cite{vanhees2015sleep}.

Because the Newcastle dataset alone was insufficient for model development, we
added DREAMT v2.2.0 \cite{wang2026dreamt,wang2024wearable}. DREAMT contains 100
participants recorded overnight in the Duke University Health System Sleep
Disorders Laboratory with simultaneous PSG and an Empatica E4 on the left wrist. The
original E4 accelerometry was sampled at 32~Hz and was supplied in a
table aligned in time at 64~Hz; expert PSG labels for sleep stages were available every
30~seconds. For the present binary task, preparation and wake were mapped to
wake, and N1, N2, N3, and REM were mapped to sleep. Only triaxial acceleration
and skin temperature were used. DREAMT reports written informed consent under
Duke Health IRB Pro00108961 \cite{wang2026dreamt}.

The initial implementation used only Newcastle data and followed our previously published
sleep/wake method \cite{mikulec2022prodromal}. Subsequent external evaluation
on DREAMT showed limited generalisation. The two development sources were
therefore pooled for XGBoost model fitting, with each participant assigned to a single partition
\cite{chen2016xgboost}. For each window, the
software calculated distributional features of acceleration magnitude,
angle relative to the vertical axis, and temperature. The resulting binary classifier returned
a sleep probability. A threshold of 0.5 defined sleep for the downstream
clinical feature pipeline.

After preprocessing, the pooled development matrix contained 267,969
windows labelled using PSG from 126 participants (28 Newcastle and 98 DREAMT).
A random split by participant (random seed 42) reserved 40\% of participants
as a final test partition: 75 participants (157,702 windows) remained for
training and 51 participants (110,267 windows) for testing. Median imputation,
hyperparameter search, and selection of the number of boosting rounds used only
the training partition, using validation with ten folds and each participant kept in one fold.

\subsection{Wearable acquisition and signal processing}\label{subsec:processing}

Raw triaxial acceleration in the two clinical collections was acquired with
GENEActiv version 1.1 devices configured at 25~Hz. Devices were
worn on the left wrist and have been validated for physical activity
measurement \cite{esliger2011validation}. Participants were asked to wear the
device for seven consecutive nights, putting it on before getting into bed and
removing it immediately after leaving bed in the morning. They were instructed
not to charge or immerse the device during the recording period and to fasten
it snugly without discomfort or looseness. Within
the intervals in bed defined by the diary, features describing acceleration
magnitude, vertical angle, and temperature were calculated for each window and
passed to the sleep/wake classifier trained using PSG labels. Predictions were
placed on a grid of 30 seconds and thresholded at 0.5. The first and last
windows labelled sleep defined the estimated sleep interval.

Nightly measures derived from actigraphy included sleep onset latency (SOL), total sleep
time (TST), time in bed (TIB), wake after sleep onset (WASO), wake after sleep offset (WASF), sleep efficiency (SE), sleep fragmentation (SF),
number of wake bouts (WB), and awakenings meeting the threshold of five minutes (WB5).

For the extended feature set, the pipeline also divided recordings into
days running from noon to noon and computed an activity index from separate
windows of 60 seconds. The daily segmentation and activity index implementation
were inspired by SleepPy \cite{christakis2019sleeppy}. Within each window, the three axes were filtered with a
Butterworth bandpass filter of order three (0.25--12~Hz). The activity index was
the square root of the mean variance across the axes, following the formulation
for raw accelerometry \cite{bai2016activityindex}. Features describing the
distribution of activity were calculated within the sleep interval estimated
by the classifier.

Participants completed a Czech sleep diary each evening
before sleep and each morning after sleep. For each of seven consecutive days,
the diary recorded daytime naps; alcohol and caffeine consumption and the time
of the last drink; use of sleep medication; time of getting into bed; time of
attempting sleep; estimated sleep latency; approximate intervals of nocturnal
awakenings; final awakening and time of leaving bed; subjective sleep quality and
morning freshness; and optional comments on unusual circumstances. Participants
were told to provide approximate times rather than monitor the clock while
falling asleep. If a day was missed, it was left blank and recording continued
on the following day.

Equivalent measures derived from diaries were retained when available. Nightly
variables from both actigraphy and diary sources were summarised across the five to seven nights using measures of
level, trend, and variability, including mean, median, minimum, maximum, slope,
standard deviation (SD), median absolute deviation (MAD), range, interquartile
range (IQR), coefficient of variation, and relative interdecile range. The core
dataset comprised 260 sleep and diary features. The extended dataset comprised
690 features and additionally summarised the distribution of the activity
index calculated each minute within each sleep window. A valid night required
an interval that could be read from the diary, from getting into bed to leaving bed,
a matching segment of recorded accelerometry, and at least one window of 30 seconds labelled sleep
within that interval. Nights without a matching recording or without any
sleep detected by the classifier were not retained. No separate algorithm was
used to exclude periods when the device was not worn within the overnight interval defined by the diary. A visit
required at least five valid nights; at most seven nights were included. The
analysis software read acceleration and temperature values from the standard
GENEActiv CSV export without additional calibration for this study.

\subsection{Clinical and cognitive outcomes}\label{subsec:outcomes}

Six clinical outcomes were available at the corresponding visit. The REM Sleep
Behaviour Disorder Screening Questionnaire score (RBDq) represented
symptoms reported by participants that were compatible with REM sleep behaviour disorder
\cite{stiasnykolster2007rbdsq}. The available Unified Parkinson's Disease
Rating Scale (UPDRS) score represented motor symptom burden; higher values were
worse \cite{fahn1987updrs}. The Mayo Fluctuations Scale (MFS) represented
characteristic cognitive fluctuation features \cite{ferman2004fluctuations}. Visuospatial, attention,
and executive measures were harmonised as domain scores, with higher values
indicating better performance. Education was harmonised as years of education. 
Recorded sex was coded female or male.

\subsection{Exploratory association analysis}\label{subsec:statistics}

We first compared age and education between groups using Welch's
$t$ tests and sex using a chi squared test. In HC versus MCI-LB, sex
($p=0.0102$) and education ($p=6.6\times10^{-6}$) passed the prespecified
$p<0.05$ imbalance rule and were selected as adjustment covariates; age did not
($p=0.1799$).

Shapiro--Wilk tests assessed the distribution of each feature separately in HC and
MCI-LB. Because most features did not follow a normal distribution in at least one group,
Mann--Whitney $U$ tests coůzsxlpoaxmpared groups and Spearman rank correlations assessed
each feature and clinical outcome in the combined HC and MCI-LB sample. The
Benjamini--Hochberg procedure controlled the false discovery rate (FDR) at 0.05
\cite{benjamini1995fdr}. The $p$ values for group comparisons were corrected across all
tested features. Correlation $p$ values were corrected separately across
features for each clinical outcome.

We reduced the large number of related features in two stages. First, each
feature was assigned to a predefined family according to its source and what it
measured. Families included long awakenings, wake bout frequency, WASO, sleep
efficiency, sleep onset latency, and activity variability. Second, within each family,
we used complete linkage clustering to group strongly correlated feature variants.
Every pair of variants in a cluster had an absolute Spearman correlation of at
least 0.85 in this cohort. One representative per
cluster was chosen by the largest absolute screening effect, followed by data
completeness, preference for robust nightly summaries, preference for a raw
rather than normalised source, and the $p$ value after FDR correction. This
selection makes the findings easier to interpret, but uses the same data as
the screening and therefore does not provide independent confirmation.

Representative associations were then examined using Gaussian GEE with an
independence working correlation and robust sandwich covariance
\cite{liang1986gee}. Each person's visits formed a cluster, so repeated visits
were accounted for when estimating uncertainty. Models included diagnosis,
sex, and education. The clinical outcome remained on its original scale, while
each feature was standardised using observations with complete data for that
model. Beta therefore represents the estimated change in the clinical outcome
for an increase of one SD in the feature. Interaction terms tested whether
the association differed between HC and MCI-LB. Confidence intervals for
Spearman $\rho$ used the same robust method, applied to standardised ranks and
with visits clustered by person. FDR
was controlled separately by outcome for adjusted main effects, interactions,
and slopes in each diagnostic group. All tests were two sided.

Sensitivity analyses tested whether the findings changed after additional
adjustment. They retained the same representative features and outcomes;
screening, clustering, and selection were not repeated. The primary GEE models were
refitted with age, clinical collection (NINR versus NU20), and both age and
collection. A further analysis replaced the collection indicator with indicators
for three recruitment groups, also called ascertainment strata: COBEN,
NU20 HC/HC2, and NU20 pre-LBD/pre-LBD2. These models were fitted with and
without age. Finally, the primary models and those adjusted for age
were repeated separately within NINR and NU20. FDR correction was
applied separately by dataset, analysis subset, model variant, and clinical
outcome. These were post hoc robustness analyses rather than independent
validation.

\subsection{Classification with validation by participant and data source}\label{subsec:classification}

Classification was a secondary analysis evaluating whether the wearable
features could distinguish the diagnostic groups. The primary analysis
examined clinical associations. Three XGBoost pipelines using boosted decision trees were evaluated
\cite{chen2016xgboost}: 
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a broad sleep/diary set with recursive feature elimination (RFE) and diary/lifestyle predictors,
  \item fixed stable sleep families with univariate $K$-best selection, and
  \item the stable sleep families plus activity variability.
\end{enumerate}
As a post hoc comparison, each feature set was also evaluated with elastic net
logistic regression. This model combines $L_1$ and $L_2$ penalties to shrink
coefficients and select variables during fitting \cite{zou2005elasticnet}.
It did not use the separate RFE or univariate $K$-best steps used by XGBoost.
The comparison therefore evaluates complete modelling strategies, including
their approaches to feature selection.
All preprocessing was fit within training folds:
linear residualisation of wearable features for sex and education, median
imputation, removal of constant features, scaling using training minima and maxima, feature selection,
hyperparameter search, and classification.

No prospective sample size calculation for prediction modelling was performed. The
classification sample comprised all available database visits with an HC or
MCI-LB label that met the requirements for valid nights and the respective pipeline.
In the core grouped table, 245 records were screened: four lacked a database
diagnosis code and 109 had other labels (79 non-HC and 30 MCI-AD), leaving
132 HC/MCI-LB visits from 118 people. The pipeline including activity features used
129 visits from 116 people because three target visits lacked usable extended
activity features. Age and sex were complete; education was missing for three
visits. Candidate columns with less than 90\% coverage were removed before
modelling. Remaining missing predictor and education values were replaced with
medians calculated within training folds.

Nested validation separated model tuning from performance evaluation. The
outer split reserved a fold for evaluation, while inner splits used the
remaining data to select settings. Both levels used five folds stratified by
diagnosis and grouped by person. All visits from the same person stayed
together, preventing a participant from appearing in both training and
validation data. Inner randomised searches selected the feature count and
XGBoost settings using balanced accuracy. To address unequal group sizes,
the weight for the positive class was searched over 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5.
No synthetic observations were generated. An optional decision threshold
maximised Matthews correlation coefficient using predictions from the inner
validation folds; the default threshold was 0.5. We report ROC AUC, the area under
the precision and recall curve (PR AUC), balanced accuracy, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), and
performance using only first visits from the validation predictions. ROC AUC
describes how well the predicted scores rank the two diagnostic groups, while
balanced accuracy gives equal weight to sensitivity and specificity. Elastic net models
used the SAGA solver, balanced class weights, and the same grouped outer and
inner folds. The inner search evaluated 30 randomly sampled combinations from
inverse regularisation strengths $C\in\{0.001,0.003,0.01,0.03,0.1,0.3,1,3,
10,30,100\}$ and $L_1$ ratios in $\{0.05,0.25,0.50,0.75,0.95,1.00\}$.

Uncertainty intervals for classification metrics used 10,000 bootstrap
samples. People were sampled with replacement within each diagnostic group,
and all visits from each sampled person were retained. We also described
calibration using the Brier score and calibration slope calculated from
validation predictions. These measures assess how well predicted probabilities
agree with observed diagnoses. We did not interpret them as evidence of clinical
utility because this analysis was not designed to provide individual risk estimates.

The comparison between models used saved predictions from the outer validation
folds for the same records and participants. For each feature set, differences
were calculated as elastic net minus XGBoost for ROC AUC, PR AUC, balanced accuracy,
MCC, Brier score, and log loss. Their 95\% percentile intervals came from
10,000 bootstrap resamples of participants within each diagnosis, retaining
all visits from each sampled person. Models were not refitted during this
bootstrap. The intervals describe differences between the saved predictions
and do not include uncertainty from refitting the models or changing the folds.
They are confidence intervals, not $p$ values.

We also fitted a logistic regression model using recruitment group alone,
with validation grouped by participant. This benchmark measured how much
information about diagnosis was already present in the recruitment design.
It could not test for recording artefacts because recruitment group was
expected to be associated with diagnosis. We repeated nested validation separately in the NINR group
and in the NU20 pre-LBD/pre-LBD2 stratum. These
analyses within each group had very unequal diagnostic group sizes:
NINR contained 46 HC and 9 MCI-LB visits, while NU20 pre-LBD/pre-LBD2 contained
11 HC and 49 MCI-LB visits. Validation was not estimable for NU20 HC/HC2 because
it contained 16 HC visits and only one MCI-LB visit. Randomised searches used
seed 17. Reporting of this secondary prediction analysis was guided by
TRIPOD+AI \cite{collins2024tripodai}; it was not intended as a deployable
clinical prediction model.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Sleep/wake classifier validation}\label{subsec:sleep-model-results}

In validation with ten folds and participants kept separate within the training partition, the
final pooled sleep/wake classifier achieved accuracy 0.70 ($\pm$0.04),
sensitivity 0.71 ($\pm$0.07), specificity 0.69 ($\pm$0.07), F1 score 0.73
($\pm$0.05), and Matthews correlation coefficient 0.40 ($\pm$0.06). On the
participants reserved for testing, accuracy was 0.72, sensitivity 0.75, specificity
0.66, F1 score 0.77, and Matthews correlation coefficient 0.41. Because both
development datasets contributed participants to the training and test
partitions, these values describe internal validation on new participants from
the combined sources. They do not show performance on an entirely new dataset.

\subsection{Cohort characteristics}\label{subsec:cohort-results}

The 132 visits represented 118 people, with 14 people contributing a second
visit. HC and MCI-LB visits had similar mean age, but the MCI-LB group contained
more women and fewer years of education (Table~\ref{tab:characteristics}). Most
visits contributed seven valid nights. As expected from the database labels,
MCI-LB visits had higher median RBDq, UPDRS, and MFS values and lower median
cognitive domain scores. Few clinical values were missing, but availability
varied by outcome, explaining small changes in sample size across analyses.

\subsection{Screening and selection of representative features}\label{subsec:screening-results}

Across the core sleep and diary dataset, 94--97\% of features did not follow a
normal distribution in at least one diagnostic group, according to the
Shapiro--Wilk tests in each group. The corresponding proportion was 87--91\%
in the extended dataset, supporting the use of ranks in screening. Of 260 core
features, 19 differed between HC and MCI-LB and 36 correlations with clinical
outcomes were significant after FDR correction. Grouping similar features
yielded 22 representative correlation clusters. Of 690 extended features, the
corresponding counts were 27 group differences, 83 correlations, and 46
representative clusters. These counts refer to calculated feature variants;
they should not be interpreted as separate biological effects.

The families showing the most consistent findings across methods were long
awakenings, sleep onset latency, sleep efficiency, and wake bout frequency.
Activity variability formed an additional family in the extended dataset.
A strong screening result for sleep onset latency normalised using the diary
was not carried forward because the corresponding raw grouped feature was
constant and its association could not be estimated in the GEE analysis.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Principal findings}\label{subsec:principal-findings}

The primary findings were associations between wearable measures and clinical
outcomes in HC and MCI-LB. Activity variability was associated with motor and
attention scores, variability in long awakenings was associated with executive
performance, and three measures of sleep continuity were associated with RBDq
mainly in MCI-LB. These associations remained after adjustment for diagnosis,
sex, and education and after accounting for repeated visits. The secondary
classification analysis showed moderate discrimination in the combined sample.
Performance was weaker and uncertain within each source, where the diagnostic
groups were highly unequal in size. The pooled results therefore do not
establish performance independent of recruitment setting.

Spearman $\rho$ and GEE beta describe different aspects of an association.
Spearman $\rho$ uses ranks to show how consistently higher feature values
accompany higher or lower outcome values. It is useful for comparing the
strength of associations across outcomes. GEE beta estimates the change in the
clinical score associated with an increase of one SD in the feature, after
accounting for diagnosis, sex, education, and repeated visits. It expresses the
association in the units of the clinical outcome. Reporting both, with
confidence intervals that account for repeated visits, shows the initial
association and the association after adjustment.

\subsection{Clinical and computational interpretation}\label{subsec:interpretation}

The activity variability features describe how movement changes within and
across nights. For the UPDRS association, the feature first measures the spread
of activity values within each night using MAD, then measures how much those
nightly MAD values vary across the week using IQR. A high value means that the
spread of nocturnal activity differs substantially from night to night.
Its positive association with UPDRS suggests that greater motor burden may
accompany less consistent patterns of rest and activity. However, actigraphy alone cannot distinguish
parkinsonian motor symptoms from awakenings, periodic limb movements,
caregiving interruptions, or environmental activity. The negative attention
association used a different summary of activity but belonged to the same
family of activity variability features. Agreement across these measures
supports examining this family further.

The executive association also concerns variation across nights. Long
awakenings were defined as at least ten consecutive windows of 30 seconds
labelled wake, giving a threshold of five minutes. The feature measures
variation across nights in the number of these awakenings, after normalisation
for age, using MAD. Higher variability was associated with poorer executive
performance. This suggests that inconsistent sleep continuity may accompany
poorer executive function, but does not show that awakenings caused cognitive
impairment. Because the normalisation depends on age, the feature should also
be compared with the raw count in an independent sample.

The RBDq interactions require especially careful interpretation. RBDq is a
symptom questionnaire, not a count of nocturnal awakenings. Wrist actigraphy
cannot identify REM sleep without physiological sleep staging and cannot
measure REM sleep without atonia. The observed positive relationships of RBDq
with wake bouts and WASO, and its negative relationship with sleep efficiency,
therefore indicate accompanying sleep disruption inferred from movement in MCI-LB,
not detection of dream enactment or proof of REM sleep behaviour disorder. The
findings are nevertheless biologically plausible and consistent with reports
of altered patterns of rest, activity, and sleep in isolated REM sleep
behaviour disorder \cite{stefani2018screening,winer2023restactivity,ophey2025accelerometry}.

Grouping features into families connected hundreds of correlated calculations
to measurements a clinician can interpret. Restricting the classifier to these
families did not improve performance. Features can therefore have interpretable
clinical associations without reliably distinguishing individual diagnoses.
Elastic net did not improve the broad model and showed poorer discrimination
than XGBoost for the restricted feature sets.

Recruitment groups had different diagnostic compositions. The moderate pooled
AUC cannot show how much of the classifier's performance reflects disease
and how much reflects other differences between those groups. The strong
performance of a model using recruitment group alone makes this limitation
visible. It does not prove that the wearable classifier learned recording
artefacts, because recruitment group was not supplied as a predictor. It does
show why pooled performance must be supported by evaluation within sources
and in independent data before generalisation can be claimed.

\subsection{Strengths and limitations}\label{subsec:limitations}

Strengths include five to seven nights per visit, explicit modelling of repeat
visits, nonparametric screening appropriate to the observed distributions,
FDR correction, reduction of correlated variants into interpretable families,
and reporting of both rank correlations and adjusted effects with robust
confidence intervals. Classification used nested validation grouped by person,
a paired comparison with elastic net, checks using first visits, evaluation
within each source, and a benchmark using recruitment group alone. The WASO
calculation was audited, and the analysis exports were regenerated after
correction.

Several limitations constrain inference. 

First, screening, feature selection, and GEE used the same relatively small
dataset. The adjusted associations remain exploratory and may overestimate
the strength of the relationships. They require independent confirmation.

Second, covariates were selected using tests of differences between groups.
Age did not meet that selection rule and was not included in the primary
models, although residual age effects, normalisation that depends on age, and other unmeasured
confounders remain possible. In the completed post hoc sensitivity analyses,
all six reported findings retained their directions after age adjustment. The
UPDRS association had a confidence interval excluding zero after adjustment
for age, but its FDR value for that outcome was 0.05108; the other pooled findings retained
FDR significance.

Third, the elastic net comparison was post hoc and compared complete modelling
strategies. Elastic net selected variables during fitting, while XGBoost used
RFE or univariate selection. Differences cannot therefore be attributed to the
classifier alone. The paired bootstrap reused saved validation predictions
without refitting either model. Its intervals do not capture variability from
new model fits or different validation folds.

Fourth, group labels and clinical variables were harmonised retrospectively
across the NU20 and NINR workbooks. The different diagnostic compositions
reflect the intended NU20 recruitment groups and the distinct NINR sampling
frame. Collections may also differ in device handling, diary completion,
assessment procedures, or other unmeasured factors. The sensitivity models
cannot distinguish technical differences from clinical differences between
collections. Estimates from pooled models adjusted for age and collection were
similar to the primary estimates, but support within each collection was
uneven and diagnostic group sizes were highly unequal. These checks reduce
concern about the influence of source without eliminating it.
% The exact recruitment, diagnostic, and acquisition protocols must
% also be documented before this draft can be submitted.

Fifth, actigraphy estimates sleep and wake from movement rather than measuring
sleep stages. No conclusions about REM sleep physiology or causation can be
drawn. 

Sixth, hundreds of summaries were generated from overlapping nightly
signals. FDR and family reduction reduce but do not eliminate researcher
degrees of freedom. The sign reversal observed for one visuospatial activity
feature between preprocessed correlation and direct adjusted modelling further
illustrates transformation sensitivity; that feature was not promoted as a
primary finding. 

Finally, there was no external cohort in which the entire processing, feature selection,
and modelling pipeline could be frozen and tested. The present classifier is
not suitable for clinical use.

Future work should preregister a compact set of raw and normalised measures
from the stable families and include age, treatment, and relevant sleep
disorders as covariates. Both diagnostic groups need adequate representation
within each source, with an independent centre or time period reserved for
validation. Analyses of repeated visits should distinguish changes within a
person from differences between people. For classification, consistent data
collection and evaluation on a centre excluded from training are priorities.
Polysomnography or additional physiological measurements would be needed to
connect fragmentation estimated from actigraphy to mechanisms specific to REM sleep.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Activity variability and sleep continuity measured during everyday life were
associated with motor, attention, executive, and RBD symptom scores in HC and
MCI-LB after adjustment for diagnosis and covariates. The associations retained
their directions in pooled models adjusted for age and source, although support
within each collection was uneven. These features merit further study as
exploratory clinical phenotypes.

Classification showed moderate performance in the combined sample, and elastic
net did not improve on XGBoost. Recruitment groups differed deliberately in
diagnostic composition, and too few participants had the less frequent diagnosis
within each source for decisive validation. Independent data with adequate
numbers of both groups within each source are required before claiming reliable
biomarkers.
