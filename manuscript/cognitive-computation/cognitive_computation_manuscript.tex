\abstract{%
\textbf{Background / introduction:} Wrist actigraphy may provide scalable
markers of sleep and activity disturbance in predementia Lewy body disease
(preDLB). We examined feature--clinical associations in healthy controls
(HC) and preDLB and audited diagnosis classification.

\textbf{Methods:} A polysomnography-referenced sleep/wake classifier was
developed from two external wearable datasets. We then analysed 132 visits from
118 people in two closed Czech clinical collections, each with five to seven
valid nights. The wrist-accelerometry pipeline produced 260 sleep/diary and
690 extended activity features. False-discovery-rate-corrected
Spearman correlations were reduced into conceptual families. Generalized
estimating equations adjusted for diagnosis, sex, and education and clustered
visits by person. Secondary XGBoost analysis used nested five-fold
person-grouped validation, within-source sensitivity analyses, and an
ascertainment-stratum-only benchmark.

\textbf{Results:} Greater activity variability was associated with higher motor
scores (adjusted beta 1.322 points per feature standard deviation, 95\% CI
0.807--1.837) and lower attention scores (beta $-0.198$, 95\% CI
$-0.311$ to $-0.084$). More variable long awakenings were associated with
lower executive scores (beta $-0.155$, 95\% CI $-0.254$ to $-0.056$).
Wake-bout frequency, sleep efficiency, and wake after sleep onset had
preDLB-specific associations with REM-sleep-behaviour symptom scores. The broad
classifier achieved ROC AUC 0.746.

\textbf{Conclusions:} Free-living activity variability and sleep continuity
show interpretable associations in HC and preDLB and merit independent
validation. The pooled data do not establish diagnostic performance independent
of their recruitment design.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) is commonly preceded by a heterogeneous
predementia phase in which cognitive, motor, sleep, autonomic, and psychiatric
features can emerge before dementia. Research criteria describe mild cognitive
impairment with Lewy bodies and delirium-onset and psychiatric-onset
presentations, while emphasising the need for longitudinal validation of early
markers \cite{mckeith2020prodromal}. Idiopathic rapid eye movement (REM) sleep
behaviour disorder is an important prodromal synucleinopathy phenotype
\cite{postuma2019risk}, whereas definitive diagnosis requires in-laboratory
polysomnography, whose resource and access demands constrain widespread
use \cite{zhou2025multicenter}.

Wrist actigraphy is inexpensive and well suited to repeated, free-living
measurement. Previous studies have shown that actigraphy can contribute to
screening for idiopathic REM sleep behaviour disorder \cite{zhou2025multicenter, stefani2018screening},
that 24-hour rest--activity measures differ in this population
\cite{winer2023restactivity}, and that accelerometry-derived sleep, physical
activity, and circadian features relate to non-motor symptoms
\cite{ophey2025accelerometry}. Recent multicentre work also demonstrates both
the promise and the difficulty of generalising actigraphy models across devices
and populations \cite{zhou2025multicenter}. Two issues therefore need to be
addressed together: whether engineered wearable features have clinically
interpretable associations, and whether apparent diagnostic performance
survives participant-grouped and source-sensitive validation.

The present exploratory study focused on healthy control (HC) and predementia
Lewy body disease (preDLB) visits assembled from multiple Czech research data
sources. We first screened a broad set of sleep, diary, and activity features
against motor, sleep-behaviour, fluctuation, visuospatial, attention, and
executive outcomes. To reduce the risk of interpreting many correlated
variants as independent discoveries, we mapped features to fixed conceptual
families, clustered highly redundant variants, and followed representative
features with person-clustered regression. We then asked whether the same
feature families supported HC-versus-preDLB classification under nested,
person-grouped validation and source-sensitive analyses.

\subsection{External datasets for sleep/wake model development}\label{subsec:sleep-model-data}

The initial development source was the Newcastle PSG--accelerometer dataset
(Zenodo record 1160410) \cite{vanhees2018newcastle}. It contains a single night
of PSG from 28 sleep-clinic patients, PSG sleep scores at 30-second resolution,
and 55 GENEActiv files recorded from both wrists at 85.7~Hz (one right-wrist
file was unavailable) \cite{vanhees2015sleep}.

Because the Newcastle dataset alone was insufficient for model development, we
added DREAMT v2.2.0 \cite{wang2026dreamt,wang2024wearable}. DREAMT contains 100
participants recorded overnight in the Duke University Health System Sleep
Disorders Laboratory with simultaneous PSG and a left-wrist Empatica E4. The
original E4 accelerometry was sampled at 32~Hz and was supplied in a
time-aligned 64-Hz table; expert PSG sleep-stage labels were available every
30~seconds. For the present binary task, preparation and wake were mapped to
wake, and N1, N2, N3, and REM were mapped to sleep. Only triaxial acceleration
and skin temperature were used. DREAMT reports written informed consent under
Duke Health IRB Pro00108961 \cite{wang2026dreamt}.

The two development sources were pooled for XGBoost model fitting with
participant-grouped splitting \cite{chen2016xgboost}. For each window, the
software calculated distributional features of acceleration magnitude,
vertical-axis angle, and temperature. The resulting binary classifier returned
a sleep probability; a threshold of 0.5 defined sleep for the downstream
clinical feature pipeline.

\subsection{Wearable acquisition and signal processing}\label{subsec:processing}

Raw triaxial acceleration in the two clinical collections was acquired with
GENEActiv version 1.1 wrist-worn devices configured at 25~Hz. Devices were
worn on the left wrist and have been validated for physical-activity
measurement \cite{esliger2011validation}. Participants were asked to wear the
device for seven consecutive nights, putting it on before getting into bed and
removing it immediately after leaving bed in the morning. They were instructed
not to charge or immerse the device during the recording period and to fasten
it snugly without discomfort or looseness. Within
diary-defined time-in-bed intervals, window-level acceleration-magnitude,
vertical-angle, and temperature features were passed to the PSG-trained sleep/wake classifier.
Predictions were placed on a 30-second grid and thresholded at 0.5. The first
and last sleep-labelled windows defined the actigraphy-inferred sleep interval.

Actigraphy-derived nightly endpoints included sleep onset latency (SOL), total sleep
time (TST), time in bed (TIB), wake after sleep onset (WASO), wake after sleep offset (WASF), sleep efficiency (SE), sleep fragmentation (SF),
number of wake bouts (WB), and awakenings meeting the five-minute threshold (WB5).

For the extended feature set, the pipeline also divided recordings into
noon-to-noon days and computed a one-minute activity index from non-overlapping
60-second windows. Within each window, the three axes were filtered with a
third-order Butterworth band-pass filter (0.25--12~Hz); the activity index was
the square root of the mean axis-wise variance, following the raw-accelerometry
activity-index formulation \cite{bai2016activityindex}. Distributional activity
features were calculated within the sleep interval defined
 by the supervised
classifier. 

Participants completed a Czech sleep diary each evening
before sleep and each morning after sleep. For each of seven consecutive days,
the diary recorded daytime naps; alcohol and caffeine consumption and the time
of the last drink; sleep-medication use; time of getting into bed; time of
attempting sleep; estimated sleep latency; approximate intervals of nocturnal
awakenings; final awakening and bed-exit times; subjective sleep quality and
morning freshness; and optional comments on unusual circumstances. Participants
were told to provide approximate times rather than monitor the clock while
falling asleep. If a day was missed, it was left blank and recording continued
on the following day.

Parallel diary-derived endpoints were retained when available. Night-level
variables from both actigraphy and diary sources were summarised across the five to seven nights using measures of
level, trend, and variability, including mean, median, minimum, maximum, slope,
standard deviation (SD), median absolute deviation (MAD), range, interquartile
range (IQR), coefficient of variation, and relative interdecile range. The core
dataset comprised 260 sleep and diary features. The extended dataset comprised
690 features and additionally summarised the distribution of the minute-level
activity index within each sleep window. A valid night required a parseable
diary-defined interval from getting into bed to leaving bed, a matching segment
of recorded accelerometry, and at least one sleep-labelled 30-second window
within that interval. Nights without a matching recording or without any
classifier-detected sleep were not retained. No separate algorithmic non-wear
exclusion was applied within the diary-defined overnight interval. A visit
required at least five valid nights; at most seven nights were included. The
analysis software read acceleration and temperature values from the standard
GENEActiv CSV export without an additional study-specific calibration step.

\subsection{Clinical and cognitive outcomes}\label{subsec:outcomes}

Six clinical outcomes were available at the corresponding visit. The REM Sleep
Behaviour Disorder Screening Questionnaire score (RBDq) represented
self-reported symptoms compatible with REM sleep behaviour disorder
\cite{stiasnykolster2007rbdsq}. The available Unified Parkinson's Disease
Rating Scale (UPDRS) score represented motor symptom burden; higher values were
worse \cite{fahn1987updrs}. The Mayo Fluctuations Scale (MFS) represented
characteristic cognitive fluctuation features \cite{ferman2004fluctuations}. Visuospatial, attention,
and executive measures were harmonised as domain scores, with higher values
indicating better performance. Education was harmonised as years of education. 
Recorded sex was coded female or male.

\subsection{Exploratory association analysis}\label{subsec:statistics}

An initial covariate-balance screen compared age and education with Welch's
two-sample $t$ tests and sex with a chi-squared test. In HC versus preDLB, sex
($p=0.0102$) and education ($p=6.6\times10^{-6}$) passed the prespecified
$p<0.05$ imbalance rule and were selected as adjustment covariates; age did not
($p=0.1799$). This rule was created during project development and was not
preregistered.

Shapiro--Wilk tests assessed each calculated feature separately in HC and
preDLB. Because most features were non-normal in at least one group, two-sided
Mann--Whitney $U$ tests compared groups and Spearman rank correlations assessed
every feature--clinical outcome pair in the pooled HC/preDLB sample. The
Benjamini--Hochberg procedure controlled the false discovery rate (FDR) at 0.05
\cite{benjamini1995fdr}. Group-comparison $p$ values were corrected across all
tested features; correlation $p$ values were corrected separately across
features for each clinical outcome.

High-dimensional variants were reduced in two stages. First, every feature was
mapped to a project-defined conceptual family based on source and measurement,   
including long awakenings, wake-bout frequency, WASO, sleep efficiency, sleep
onset latency, and activity variability. Second, within each conceptual family,
complete-linkage clustering grouped variants whose absolute within-cohort
Spearman correlation was at least 0.85. One representative per redundancy
cluster was chosen by the largest absolute screening effect, followed by data
completeness, preference for robust nightly summaries, preference for a raw
rather than normalised source, and FDR-adjusted $p$ value. This reduction aids
interpretation but is data-driven and does not constitute replication.

Representative associations were followed with Gaussian generalised
estimating equations (GEE) using an independence working correlation and robust
sandwich covariance \cite{liang1986gee}. Models clustered visits by underlying
person and included diagnosis, sex, and education. The clinical outcome
remained on its original scale, while each feature was standardised within the
model's complete-case sample; beta therefore represents outcome change per one
feature SD. Feature-by-diagnosis interactions tested whether slopes differed
between HC and preDLB. Spearman-rho confidence intervals were estimated with
the same person-clustered robust framework applied to standardised ranks. FDR
was controlled separately by outcome for adjusted main effects, interactions,
and diagnosis-specific slopes. All tests were two-sided.

Sensitivity analyses retained the representative feature--outcome pairs chosen
by the canonical screening and family-reduction pipeline; screening, clustering,
and representative selection were not repeated. The primary GEE models were
refitted with age, clinical collection (NINR versus NU20), and both age and
collection. A secondary ascertainment audit replaced the collection indicator
with three recruitment-stratum indicators (COBEN, NU20 HC/HC2, and NU20
pre-LBD/pre-LBD2), with and without age. Finally, the primary and age-adjusted
models were repeated separately within NINR and NU20. FDR correction was
applied separately by dataset, analysis subset, model variant, and clinical
outcome. These were post hoc robustness analyses rather than independent
validation.

\subsection{Person-grouped classification and source-sensitive validation}\label{subsec:classification}

Classification was a secondary validity analysis, not the primary clinical
claim. Three XGBoost gradient-boosted tree pipelines were evaluated
\cite{chen2016xgboost}: (1) a broad sleep/diary set with recursive feature
elimination (RFE) and diary/lifestyle predictors, (2) fixed stable sleep
families with univariate $K$-best selection, and (3) the stable sleep families
plus activity variability. All preprocessing was fit within training folds:
linear residualisation of wearable features for sex and education, median
imputation, removal of constant features, min--max scaling, feature selection,
hyperparameter search, and classification. The RFE search considered 20, 40,
80, or 120 features; $K$-best considered 20, 40, 80, 120, or all available
features.

Outer and inner validation both used five-fold stratified group splits by
underlying person, preventing first and later visits from appearing in
different folds. Hyperparameters and an optional probability threshold were
selected only from each outer training partition. Performance measures were
area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC AUC), area under
the precision--recall curve (PR AUC), balanced accuracy, sensitivity,
specificity, precision, and Matthews correlation coefficient. We additionally
reported first-visit-only performance from the held-out predictions.

\subsection{Screening and feature-family reduction}\label{subsec:screening-results}

Across the core sleep/diary dataset, 94--97\% of features were non-normal in at
least one diagnostic group, depending on the group-specific Shapiro--Wilk
criterion. The corresponding proportion was 87--91\% in the extended dataset,
supporting rank-based screening. Of 260 core features, 19 showed FDR-significant
HC/preDLB group differences and 36 feature--outcome correlations were
FDR-significant; reduction yielded 22 representative correlation clusters. Of
690 extended features, the corresponding counts were 27 group differences, 83
correlations, and 46 representative clusters. These are counts of engineered
variants, not independent biological effects.

The most coherent cross-method families were long awakenings, sleep onset
latency, sleep efficiency, and wake-bout frequency. Activity
variability formed an additional family in the extended dataset. A strong
diary-normalised sleep-onset-latency screening result was also downgraded
because the corresponding raw grouped feature was constant and could not be
estimated in direct GEE follow-up.

\subsection{Shared adjusted clinical associations}\label{subsec:shared-results}

Three representative associations combined FDR-significant rank screening
with a consistent diagnosis-adjusted GEE result (Table~\ref{tab:shared}).
Greater between-night dispersion of nightly activity MAD was associated with
higher UPDRS scores (pooled $\rho=0.331$; adjusted beta 1.322 UPDRS points per
feature SD; Fig.~\ref{fig:activity-updrs}). Greater variability in relative
interdecile activity range was associated with lower attention scores
($\rho=-0.327$; adjusted beta $-0.198$). More variable age-normalised counts of
awakenings longer than five minutes were associated with lower executive
scores ($\rho=-0.324$; adjusted beta $-0.155$;
Fig.~\ref{fig:awakenings-executive}). None of these three feature-by-diagnosis
interactions survived FDR correction, supporting a shared adjusted slope in the
combined sample rather than clear evidence for different HC and preDLB slopes.

\subsection{Person-grouped classification and source-sensitive validation}\label{subsec:classification-results}

Under nested person-grouped cross-validation, the broad RFE model provided
moderate ranking performance (ROC AUC 0.746, PR AUC 0.744) and balanced
accuracy of 0.649 at the default threshold or 0.666 with inner-fold threshold
tuning (Table~\ref{tab:classification}). Restricting input to the stable sleep
families reduced ROC AUC to 0.706, while adding activity variability reduced it
further to 0.620. First-visit-only AUC values were similar or lower, indicating
that repeat-visit leakage did not explain the broad model's entire apparent
signal.

The ascertainment-stratum-only benchmark achieved ROC AUC 0.814 and balanced
accuracy 0.840. Diagnosis and stratum were strongly associated (Cramer's
$V=0.682$, $p=4.65\times10^{-14}$), consistent with the consortium-defined
diagnosis-enriched recruitment strata. The broad model's within-stratum AUC was
0.579 in NINR and 0.501 in NU20 pre-LBD/pre-LBD2; corresponding values for the
stable sleep model were 0.522 and 0.510. These estimates provide limited
evidence for transportable discrimination within either stratum, but their
precision and interpretation are constrained by the small minority classes.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Principal findings}\label{subsec:principal-findings}

This exploratory analysis identified clinically interpretable wearable
phenotypes in HC and preDLB while also demonstrating why association evidence
must not be conflated with diagnostic prediction. Across correlated feature
variants, the most coherent findings involved activity variability, long
awakenings, wake-bout frequency and sleep efficiency. Activity
variability was associated with motor and attention outcomes, variability in
long awakenings was associated with executive performance, and three
sleep-continuity measures showed RBDq associations concentrated in preDLB.
These effects survived adjustment for diagnosis, sex, and education in
person-clustered models. The person-grouped classifier showed moderate pooled
discrimination, but the diagnosis-enriched source composition and imprecise,
weak within-source results prevented a source-independent performance claim.

The pooled Spearman coefficient and adjusted GEE beta answer complementary
questions. Spearman $\rho$ quantifies the strength and direction of a monotonic
relationship on a standardised scale and is useful for comparing associations
across outcomes. The GEE beta estimates the expected change in the original
clinical outcome for a one-SD increase in a feature after accounting for
diagnosis, sex, education, and repeated visits. Beta therefore evaluates
whether the relationship persists under adjustment and expresses its clinical
scale, but it is not itself a correlation coefficient. Reporting both, with
person-clustered confidence intervals, avoids treating adjusted effect size and
unadjusted rank association as interchangeable.

\subsection{Clinical and computational interpretation}\label{subsec:interpretation}

The activity-variability features describe inconsistency rather than simply
high or low activity. For example, the UPDRS feature first computes the median
absolute deviation of minute-level activity within each night and then takes
the IQR of that nightly value across the recording week. A high value therefore
indicates fluctuating nocturnal movement dispersion across nights. Its positive
association with UPDRS is compatible with greater motor burden accompanying a
less stable rest--activity phenotype, but actigraphy alone cannot distinguish
parkinsonian motor symptoms from awakenings, periodic limb movements,
caregiving interruptions, or environmental activity. The negative attention
association used a different distributional summary yet converged on the same
broad activity-variability family, which is more compelling than reliance on a
single engineered column.

The executive association similarly concerns between-night instability. Long
awakenings were operationalised from runs of at least ten contiguous 30-second
windows labelled wake, corresponding to the pipeline's five-minute threshold.
The MAD across
nights of the age-normalised count was negatively associated with executive
performance. This may indicate that inconsistent sleep continuity co-occurs
with poorer executive function. It does not establish that awakenings caused
cognitive impairment, and the normalised feature incorporates an age-dependent
transformation that should be tested against raw endpoints in an independent
sample.

The RBDq interactions require especially careful interpretation. RBDq is a
symptom questionnaire, not a count of nocturnal awakenings. Wrist actigraphy
cannot identify REM sleep without physiological sleep staging and cannot
measure REM sleep without atonia. The observed positive relationships of RBDq
with wake bouts and WASO, and its negative relationship with sleep efficiency,
therefore indicate co-occurring movement-defined sleep disruption in preDLB,
not detection of dream enactment or proof of REM sleep behaviour disorder. The
findings are nevertheless biologically plausible and consistent with reports
of altered rest--activity and sleep characteristics in isolated REM sleep
behaviour disorder \cite{stefani2018screening,winer2023restactivity,ophey2025accelerometry}.

From a cognitive-computation perspective, conceptual feature families provided
a useful bridge between hundreds of correlated calculations and mechanisms
that a clinician can inspect. However, family restriction did not improve
classification. This is informative: interpretability constraints can expose
stable association domains without guaranteeing individual-level diagnostic
separation. Because source and diagnosis were structurally entangled by the
recruitment design, the broad classifier's moderate pooled AUC cannot determine
how much discrimination arose from disease-related wearable signal versus
wearable differences associated with the recruitment strata. The high
ascertainment-stratum-only benchmark does not prove that the wearable classifier
learned technical source artefacts, because source identity was not supplied as a
predictor. It does show that pooled classification performance alone is
insufficient to establish transportability beyond this study design.

\subsection{Strengths and limitations}\label{subsec:limitations}

Strengths include five to seven nights per visit, explicit modelling of repeat
visits, nonparametric screening appropriate to the observed distributions,
FDR correction, reduction of correlated variants into interpretable families,
and reporting of both rank correlations and adjusted effects with robust
confidence intervals. The classification audit used true person-grouped nested
validation, first-visit sensitivity, within-source evaluation, and a simple
ascertainment-stratum-only benchmark. The WASO implementation was audited and all
canonical exports were regenerated after correction rather than retaining a
weakened result.

Several limitations constrain inference. 

First, screening, family reduction,
and GEE follow-up used the same relatively small dataset. The GEE results are
therefore adjusted exploratory follow-up, not independent confirmation, and
effect sizes may be optimistic. 

Second, covariates were selected by a
data-driven imbalance test. Age did not cross that rule and was not included,
although residual age effects, age-dependent normalisation, and other unmeasured
confounders remain possible. In the completed post hoc sensitivity analyses,
all six reported findings retained their directions after age adjustment. The
UPDRS association had an age-adjusted confidence interval excluding zero but
its within-outcome FDR value was 0.05108; the other pooled findings retained
FDR significance.

Third, group labels and clinical variables were
harmonised retrospectively across the NU20 and NINR source workbooks. The
strong differences in diagnosis composition were an intended consequence of
the NU20 recruitment strata and the distinct NINR sampling frame, not
evidence of an acquisition error. Nevertheless, collection and ascertainment
stratum may
also be associated with device handling, diary completion, assessment
procedures, or other unmeasured factors. The sensitivity models cannot
distinguish technical from clinical collection differences. Pooled age and collection adjusted
estimates were similar to the primary estimates, but within-collection support
was uneven and the diagnostic groups were highly imbalanced within each source.
The sensitivity analyses therefore reduce, but do not eliminate, concern about
source-dependent inference.

Fourth, actigraphy infers movement-defined sleep and wake rather than measuring
sleep stages. No conclusions about REM sleep physiology or causation can be
drawn. 

Fifth, hundreds of summaries were generated from overlapping nightly
signals. FDR and family reduction reduce but do not eliminate researcher
degrees of freedom. The sign reversal observed for one visuospatial activity
feature between preprocessed correlation and direct adjusted modelling further
illustrates transformation sensitivity; that feature was not promoted as a
primary finding. 

Finally, there was no external cohort in which the entire processing, feature selection,
and modelling pipeline could be frozen and tested. The present classifier is
not suitable for clinical use.

Future work should preregister a compact set of raw and normalised endpoints
from the stable families, include age and relevant treatment or sleep-disorder
covariates, recruit sufficient numbers of both diagnostic groups within each
source, and reserve an
independent centre or time period for validation. Repeated-measures analyses
should distinguish within-person change from cross-sectional between-person
differences. For classification, prospective harmonisation and leave-one-centre-
out validation are more important than additional feature selection. For
clinical interpretation, polysomnography or multimodal physiology would be
needed to connect actigraphy-derived fragmentation to REM-specific mechanisms.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

In free-living HC and preDLB observations, interpretable activity-variability
and sleep-continuity families were associated with motor, attention, executive,
and RBD symptom measures after diagnosis and covariate adjustment. The findings
were directionally stable in pooled age and source adjusted analyses, although
within-collection support was uneven. They support wearable feature families as exploratory phenotypes for clinical
association research. They do not yet establish diagnostic classification
independent of study design: diagnosis was deliberately enriched across
ascertainment strata, and the minority classes were too small for decisive within-source
validation. Independent validation with adequate representation of both groups
within each source is required before either biomarker or clinical-utility
claims are warranted.
